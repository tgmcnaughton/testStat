Tutorial: how to interpret the diagram

Binary classification means that a test returns one of two qualitative results: yes/no, positive/negative, or present/absent.

For a perfectly accurate diagnostic test, everyone with disease tests positive (100% sensitivity) and everyone without disease tests negative (100% specificity). Real-world tests make two kinds of errors: a false positive is a false alarm, while a false negative is a missed alarm.

The diagram represents a 2×2 contingency table. The left side contains patients with disease and the right side healthy patients. The top represents positive tests and the bottom negative tests. Each colored rectangle's area is proportional to the number of patients in that group.

Predictive values

PPV answers: “Given that this patient's test is positive, how likely is it that the patient actually has the disease?” NPV answers the corresponding question after a negative result.

Try lowering prevalence while keeping sensitivity and specificity high. The false-positive rectangle can become a large fraction of all positive tests even though the test itself is highly accurate.

Screening and confirmatory testing

Screening is difficult in low-prevalence populations. A highly sensitive first test can identify most people with disease, but may also produce many false positives. A more specific confirmatory test can then be applied to the test-positive group. Clinical history and physical examination likewise change the pre-test probability before a formal diagnostic test is interpreted.

Using the App to Extend Reported Diagnostic Statistics

Diagnostic studies frequently report selected measures of test performance, such as sensitivity, specificity, and disease prevalence, while omitting clinically important derived measures such as positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV). This application can be used to extend the reported data by entering the available test characteristics and calculating the corresponding predictive values and expected frequencies of true-positive, false-positive, true-negative, and false-negative results. Displaying these outcomes for a defined hypothetical population also provides a direct visual representation of how test performance interacts with disease prevalence in the population being studied.
